Section 105 · Data: How Many Bytes Per Second

Two Substitute Quantities That Can Be Verified

A Lifetime of Bytes (C)

About 3 GB of network traffic per person per day (whole-population average) comes to about 80 TB over a lifetime (at current rates, C). The great majority of these bytes are video, not things people wrote or remembered. For comparison: this ledger's table of constants estimates the long-term memory of one person over a lifetime at about 125 MB (B).

The retinal ganglion cell layer sends out about 10⁷ bits per second, and over a waking lifetime about 1.5 × 10¹⁶ bits enter the retina (Koch et al. 2006, Curr Biol 16:1428; Inference). The output of consciousness and behavior is about 10 bits per second (Zheng & Meister 2025, Neuron 113:192). What is written into long-term memory is about 2 bits per second, about 10⁹ bits over a lifetime (Landauer 1986), roughly one ten-millionth of the amount that enters.

The Gap in Basis Between Total Data and Broadband Traffic

"Data created, captured, copied, and consumed each year" is about 175 zettabytes (2025), from a forecast made in 2018 by IDC's Global DataSphere (another estimate of the same period exceeds 200 ZB), and is not a measurement. The DataSphere counts every copy of the same data once, while broadband traffic of 279 TB per second, about 8.8 ZB a year, counts only bytes that have been moved, so traffic is only one twentieth of the DataSphere.

Section 119H · Everything Humans Have Written: How Many Bytes, How Many Tokens

Before the accounting, count how much humans have written down to this day. This section converts the whole stock of human literature into two units: how many bytes to store it, and how many tokens a machine must read.

The token is the one measure in the ledger that points directly at machines. One token is about 0.75 of an English word, or 1.5 Chinese characters; today's large models are priced by the token and trained by the token. Converting human knowledge into tokens means calculating how much has to be fed in to feed in everything humans have written.

Humans have published 130 million distinct books (Google's 2010 deduplicated count, A). One book's plain text is about 1 MB (about 250,000 Chinese characters), so:

All the books humanity has written and published since it had writing fit, as plain text, on one 130 TB hard disk: the ordinary configuration of a server today, with a cabinet holding several dozen copies.

By comparison with the data section: the data created, captured, copied, and consumed worldwide in a year come to about 175 zettabytes (1.75×10²³ bytes, C).

All of humanity's books ÷ one year's flood of data ＝ about one in 1.3 billion.

The data the world produces every 0.02 seconds equals all the books ever published in human history. The great majority of data is the Nth copy of the same video; what humans spent five thousand years writing has, in quantity, been entirely drowned in the noise they make themselves.

Error and Uncounted

  • 130 million titles is a 2010 figure, not updated in fifteen years (no newer figure exists at present); it is deduplicated by "work" and excludes unpublished manuscripts, government archives, periodicals, and web text.
  • "One book is 1 MB / 100,000 tokens" is an order-of-magnitude assumption (C): Chinese books have about 1.4 times as many tokens as English ones, and academic books are far larger than novels. With 0.5 MB or 2 MB, the conclusion (the ratio to one year's data volume) stays in the same order of magnitude.
  • The cumulative 150 million papers is a rough estimate (C).
  • The text volume of web pages, social media, and audio and video is not collected in the ledger: unverified.
  • 175 ZB is IDC's forecast basis and includes copies, which differs entirely from the original-work basis for "books"; comparing the two shows only that the orders of magnitude are far apart and carries no value judgment.

Section 119I · Memory: 125 MB Per Person, One Exabyte for Eight Billion

The Part That Was Not Written Down

What was not written down is held in living brains.

Basis: Landauer's classic 1986 estimate, an adult's long-term memory is about 10⁹ bits, about 125 MB, with a write rate of about 2 bits per second (B). This figure is disputed in the field (some estimate above 10¹⁰ bits), but it is one of the few estimates that are cited repeatedly with a clear basis, and this ledger uses it too.

Memory is written on synapses. When two neurons fire together repeatedly, transmission across the synapse between them strengthens; this is called long-term potentiation, first recorded in the rabbit hippocampus (Bliss & Lømo 1973). New facts and experiences depend first on the hippocampus and are then transferred to the cerebral cortex over weeks to years, with sleep taking part in this step (Kandel, Principles of Neural Science). In 1953 the patient H.M. had the medial temporal lobes on both sides, together with the hippocampus, removed; afterward he could not retain new things, while events from many years before the operation and newly learned manual skills remained (Scoville & Milner 1957).

The molecular steps of long-term potentiation: glutamate + postsynaptic depolarization → NMDA receptors lose their Mg²⁺ block → Ca²⁺ flows in → CaMKII is activated → AMPA receptors are inserted into the postsynaptic membrane (Kandel, Principles of Neural Science). An adult has about 1.5–3 × 10¹⁴ synapses (Pakkenberg et al. 2003; Tang et al. 2001), and the size of a single synapse can be resolved into about 26 levels, about 4.7 bits (Bartol et al. 2015, eLife 4:e10778, rat hippocampus). Multiplying the two gives a physical upper limit of about 0.7–1.4 × 10¹⁵ bits, about six orders of magnitude above the 10⁹ bits of functional memory.

What one person remembers in a lifetime is about equal to 125 books. Seventy-three years, twelve years of school, tens of thousands of hours of work, countless conversations and journeys, compressed into one small USB stick.

The eight billion present add up to about 1 exabyte; the 117 billion in all of history add up to about 15 exabytes. The data the world produces in a year is 175 zettabytes: everything that has ever been held in all human brains in all of history is less than one 12,000th of the world's data volume for one year today.

Link 6: about 62.4 million people die each year, and the long-term memory that disappears with them is about 7.8 petabytes (PB). Every second, humanity loses about 250 million bytes of memory, about the whole of what two people remember in a lifetime.

Before Link 2 there is a narrow door: working memory holds only about 4 ± 1 chunks at a time (Cowan 2001, BBS 24:87), and without rehearsal it scatters in about 15–30 seconds (Peterson & Peterson 1959). The 39 bits/second in Link 3 is an average across 17 languages, at about 4–9 syllables per second (Coupé et al. 2019, Sci Adv 5:eaaw2594).

Error and Uncounted

  • 125 MB comes from a 1986 estimate and is the order of magnitude of functional memory, not the physical capacity of neural connections (estimates of the latter are higher by several orders of magnitude). It cannot be measured directly: Grade B, and heavily disputed.
  • The "converted to books" comparison treats memory as text, which is a simplification: memory holds movements, faces, smells, and skills, which are not equivalent to bytes. This conversion is used only to give the order of magnitude a shape that can be imagined, and not for any inference.
  • This section has two bases for converting a lifetime of memory to tokens and books, and they coexist. Byte basis: 125 MB as plain English text, about 4 bytes per token, gives about 31 million tokens, and at 1 MB per book, about 125 books; the two conversion tables in this section use this one. Information basis: 10⁹ bits, with each token carrying about 4–12 bits, gives about 83–250 million tokens, and at about 138,000 tokens per book, about 600–1,800 books; the constants table and the data center section use this one. The books in the previous section were converted at 100,000 tokens each, yet another basis; the three places are compared only in order of magnitude.
  • The scale of large-model training corpora is industry hearsay that no company publishes: C.
  • "How much disappears with the person" assumes every dead person had an adult's full memory, ignoring infant deaths and cognitive decline: C.
