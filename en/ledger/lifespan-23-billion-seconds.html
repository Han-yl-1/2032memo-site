Lifespan (32 to 73.3 Years) & How 2.3 Billion Seconds Are Spent (Sections 8–9)

Section 8 · Lifespan Is Not One Number

The largest batch of people, and one thing each holds in equal amount: a day of 86,400 seconds.

Volume Two · Following One Second

Follow one second through the time accounts.

One person's second, magnified 8.162 billion times, is civilization's second; multiplied by 86,400, it is 195.9 billion person-hours a day; multiplied by 73.3 years, it is one person's lifetime: 2.3 billion seconds.

Of these 2.3 billion seconds, about a third is slept away; averaged over the whole population, about a tenth goes to work counted in GDP, about a twelfth to unpaid care and housework, and about a twentieth to travel. The remaining forty-odd percent is eating, schooling, playing, scrolling, talking, and spacing out, the largest block of a lifetime, which no global statistic can cut apart.

Adding up every living person's remaining lifespan, this batch of humanity has about 358.5 billion person-years left, an average of 44 years each, more than half of which is held by people under 25. Each passing day, this balance loses 8.162 billion person-days; at the same time, medicine pushes life expectancy up by about 0.36 years each year, topping up the balance. All the figures of this volume answer the same question: where this balance is being spent.

Five different numbers for "how long can one live"

"How long can a person live" has at least five different numbers: life expectancy at birth, remaining life expectancy at each age, life expectancy by sex, healthy life expectancy, and maximum lifespan. Here the first four are pinned down, and maximum lifespan is given only as a boundary note.

Boundary: all life expectancies are period life expectancies, meaning how many years one would live if one spent a whole life under this year's death rates at each age. It is not a forecast for any real birth cohort. Medical progress will let real cohorts live longer than the period life expectancy.

The global values for 1800 and 1900 in the table come from historical reconstruction (Riley, Zijdeman et al.), Grade B. Since 1950, for each year that passes, humanity's average life expectancy at birth has risen by about 0.36 years: roughly one extra day for every 2.7 days that pass.

Remaining life expectancy at 65 is changing too: 11.38 years in 1950, 17.57 years in 2023, 6.2 years more over seventy-odd years. This is the joint account of geriatric medicine, chronic disease management, and living conditions.

In one person's lifetime, about 13% of the time is spent in illness or disability. These 9.5 years are the main population served by the medical system and by care labor.

Maximum lifespan

The longest-lived person with reliable records is Jeanne Calment of France (1875–1997), 122 years and 164 days; Calment's age has been checked many times, and some individual researchers have questioned it, and the ledger follows Guinness and mainstream demography in accepting it. Life expectancy is rising, but the recorded maximum lifespan has not been broken in thirty years. Whether an upper limit to human lifespan exists has so far no settled answer.

Cells carry a counter of their own. Human fibroblasts in a culture dish stop after dividing about 40–60 times, called the Hayflick limit (Hayflick 1965, Experimental Cell Research). At the ends of chromosomes are telomeres; human telomeres are repeats of the six bases TTAGGG, and each cell division shortens them by a little; germ cells and most cancer cells restore them with telomerase (Blackburn, Greider, Szostak, 2009 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine).

A person's telomeres are about 10–15 kilobases long at birth, each division shortening them by about 50–200 bases (Aubert & Lansdorp 2008). Of the O₂ consumed by the respiratory chain, about 0.1%–0.2% leaks out as the superoxide anion O₂•⁻ (the old estimate was 1%–2%); it oxidizes DNA, lipids, and proteins, and too much of it pushes aging (Murphy 2009, Biochem J 417:1; St-Pierre et al. 2002, J Biol Chem 277:44784).

Section 9 · The Stopwatch of a Lifetime: How 2.3 Billion Seconds Are Spent

Converting a lifetime into seconds

73.3 years, written in seconds, is 2.3 billion. This is all the raw material one person can dispose of.

This section converts life expectancy into seconds, minutes, hours, and days, then into the beats of the body (heartbeats, breaths) and the beats of living (eating, sleeping, going to work). It is the summary table of "one person's accounts."

Boundary: this "lifetime" takes the 2024 life expectancy at birth of 73.3 years (A). Individual differences are enormous; this table is the lifetime of an average person, not of any one person.

The beats of the body (Grade C basis)

BeatBasisLifetime totalWhat it is
HeartbeatAverage 70 beats/minuteAbout 2.7 billion beatsThe sinoatrial node in the right atrium spontaneously generates rhythmic excitation that drives the heart muscle to contract; at rest about 5 liters of blood are pumped per minute; a resting rate below 60 beats/minute is bradycardia, above 100 beats/minute is tachycardia
BreathingAverage 15 breaths/minuteAbout 580 million breathsThe diaphragm contracts and draws air into the alveoli, O₂ enters the blood, CO₂ leaves the blood; at rest about 500 ml per breath (tidal volume); inhaled air is about 21% oxygen, exhaled about 16%
BlinkingNo statistics—The eyelids close and re-spread the tear film over the cornea

Heart rate and breathing rate vary greatly with age, activity, and sleep (an infant's heart rate is over 100, an adult's at rest 60–80), and the above gives only the order of magnitude. The number of heartbeats in a person's lifetime is of the same order as the number of seconds in the lifetime.

The speed of the heartbeat is regulated by the autonomic nervous system: sympathetic nerves speed it up, the vagus slows it down. At rest the vagus has the upper hand; with both blocked, the heart beats at the sinoatrial node's own rhythm, about 118 − 0.57 × age beats per minute, about 95 at age forty (Jose & Collison 1970, Cardiovascular Research 4:160, B).

At about 5 liters of blood pumped per minute at rest, about 500 ml per breath, and 15 breaths per minute, over a lifetime of 38.55 million minutes the heart pumps about 190 million liters of blood and the lungs move about 290 million liters of air (for cardiac output and tidal volume, see Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology). Heartbeats and breaths are order-of-magnitude conversions and are not stated as fact.

The ATP made and used in a day is about 80 moles, about 40 kilograms, comparable in order of magnitude to body weight (converted from Lehninger and Törnroth-Horsefield & Neutze 2008). Multiplying the daily amount by 26,772 days, over a lifetime about 27 tons of CO₂ are exhaled and about 8.8 × 10¹⁵ cells are renewed (converted from Sender & Milo 2021, Nature Medicine 27:45).

"Work counted in GDP" is the whole-population average, with children, the elderly, and the non-working all averaged in. An employed person works far more than this: employed people worldwide average 42 hours a week (Gethin & Saez 2025, A). A person employed continuously from age 20 to 60, at 42 hours a week and 50 weeks a year, works about 84,000 hours in a lifetime, about 3,500 days, 9.6 years. The 8 hours of sleep in the table is the ledger's basis (C); measured sleep for adults is mostly 6.5–7.5 hours, and longer for children; the "rest" column is a remainder, and different time-use surveys classify differently; the section on where time goes gives the parts that can be checked.
