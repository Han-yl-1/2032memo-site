What One Person Eats in a Day & Global Hunger vs. Obesity (Sections 27 & 32)

Section 27 · What One Person Eats in a Day

Every day 170,000 people go offline and no longer eat; 8 billion mouths are still here.

Volume 4 · Following a Grain of Wheat

Follow one grain of wheat through the food ledger once.

Worldwide, about 99 tons of grain are harvested every second; spread over each person, that is about 1 kg a day. But a big share of that 1 kg goes into the stomachs of livestock, the vats of ethanol plants and the seed stores. Every second 2,489 chickens are slaughtered, and they eat another part of the grain.

From harvest to before retail, grains and pulses lose 8.4% and fruit and vegetables 25.4%; at retail, in restaurants and at home, about 19% of the available food is thrown away again — 1.052 billion tons a year, six tenths of it at home.

At the end of the road, the dietary energy supply per person per day is about 2,978 kcal, already above the average requirement. Yet at the same time 673 million people are chronically hungry, 2.6 billion cannot afford a healthy diet, and 150 million children under five are stunted; at the other end, 890 million adults are obese. Those who grow this food are 928 million agricultural workers — each supporting 8.8 people on average — and they themselves are among the poorest groups in the world. The accounts of this volume show that the problem of food lies not in the total but on the road and in the distribution.

A Day's Food Is Counted at Three Levels

A bowl of rice on the table: from the field to the mouth, the ledger has to record it three times.

A person's food for one day is booked at three levels: how much is produced (in the fields, pens and water), how much is supplied to the table (after subtracting feed, seed, industrial use and losses, averaged per person), and how much is actually eaten (intake). The three grow smaller in that order, and mixing them up is the most common error in food accounting.

At the production level, each person has about 1 kg of grain a day, of which only about 410 g is eaten directly (food use 41.7%, A).

Supply Level: 2,978 kcal

FAO food balance sheets: in 2021 the global dietary energy supply per person was 2,978 kcal/day. For all of humanity the total is about 24.3 trillion kcal a day.

An ordinary adult's energy requirement is between 2,000 and 2,500 kcal (FAO/WHO, B); children and the elderly need less. So the supply level already exceeds the average requirement of all humanity — hunger is not that the total falls short; it is that the food does not reach people.

2,978 kcal is about 12.5 MJ; spread over the 86,400 seconds of a day, it is a continuous power of about 144 W — that much chemical energy a person's body has to burn every second. For all of humanity, about 24.3 trillion kcal a day is about 1.02 × 10¹⁷ J, equivalent to about 1.2 × 10¹² W. The energy comes from three fuels: carbohydrate and protein at about 4 kcal per gram, fat at about 9 kcal per gram; oxidized in cells through aerobic respiration, C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O, the energy released is stored in ATP and then used up.

Of this, the base figure that sustains life is basal metabolism: for a 70 kg man about 1.6–1.8 × 10³ kcal/day, about 80 joules per second (Schofield equation, FAO/WHO/UNU 2004). It is distributed as liver about 21%, brain about 20%, skeletal muscle about 22%, heart about 9%, kidney about 8%, and the remaining about 20% (Elia 1992). At rest, a day consumes about 360 liters of O₂, and each liter of O₂ corresponds to about 20.1 kJ, about 7.2 × 10³ kJ in all, consistent with basal metabolism (Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology).

The Drop in a Bowl of Rice

The rim of the bowl is hot. The starch in the rice is cut short in the mouth by salivary amylase and cut into glucose in the small intestine, C₆H₁₂O₆, six carbons, twelve hydrogens, six oxygens, which passes through the intestinal wall into the blood.

The energy in this molecule has come a long way. A rice plant in the field assembled it out of six CO₂ and six H₂O, taking in about 2,870 kJ of light energy per mole and giving off six O₂. Each time chlorophyll takes in a photon of about 680 nm, about 1.8 eV, it knocks an electron up to a high energy level; from there the electron falls all the way down, and the drop is all the energy that follows. Every grain of rice in the bowl is a small stretch of stored sunshine.

The whole carbon-based agent starts running from here. There is only one way of running, repeated a dozen-odd times below: spend energy to pile things up on one side, hold them back with a conditional gate; when the gate opens, they flow down the drop, doing work or passing a signal as they flow.

First Drop: Electrons Fall from Sugar to Oxygen

On the C–H bonds of glucose, the electrons sit high up. The oxygen atom holds electrons most tightly, and when electrons fall onto oxygen the energy is released. One mole of glucose falling completely gives ΔG°′ of about −2,840 kJ.

Falling all at once would set it on fire. The cell breaks this step into more than twenty small steps. Glycolysis in the cytosol first splits the six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon pyruvates, releasing only about 3% of the total energy, for a net gain of 2 ATP. Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, where carbons are stripped off one by one, become CO₂, travel with the blood to the lungs and are breathed out. A day's exhaled CO₂ is about one kilogram, and most of the part of body weight that is burned off leaves from here.

When carbons are stripped off, the electrons are not lost; they are taken up by two carrier molecules: NAD⁺ becomes NADH, and FAD becomes FADH₂. One glucose hands over 10 NADH and 2 FADH₂. The real energy is still on the electrons at this point, waiting to fall.

The drop can be written in volts. The standard potential at the NADH end is about −0.32 V, and at the oxygen end about +0.82 V, a difference of about 1.14 V. ΔG = −nFΔE: two electrons times the Faraday constant of 96.5 kC per mole, times 1.14 V, gives about −220 kJ per mole. The electrons hop down the inner mitochondrial membrane one step at a time, releasing a small portion at each step. The mode of hopping is quantum tunneling: from one iron–sulfur cluster or one heme to the next, with protein in between, and they can make the jump only within about 1.4 nm; farther than that, the rate falls off exponentially.

Second Drop: Protons Piled Up Outside the Membrane

Embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane are three respiratory chain complexes, numbered I, III and IV. Each time electrons pass one, that complex pushes H⁺ from the matrix side to the intermembrane space side. One pair of electrons falling from NADH to oxygen pushes about 10 H⁺ all the way along. When the electrons reach the bottom they combine with oxygen and protons to make water: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O. The oxygen breathed in becomes water inside the body here.

As more and more H⁺ piles up in the intermembrane space, an electrochemical potential of about 150–200 mV opens up across the inner membrane. This potential is made of two parts: the electric potential difference Δψ and the H⁺ concentration difference ΔpH. Δp = Δψ − (2.3RT/F)ΔpH; at 37 °C, 2.3RT/F is about 61.5 mV, and in mitochondria most of it lies in the electric potential difference.

These 0.1-odd volts fall across an oil layer only about 3 nm thick. The field strength E = V/d, 0.15 V divided by 3 nm, is about 5 × 10⁷ V/m. Dry air breaks down at about 3 × 10⁶ V/m and strikes lightning; the field strength on the membrane is more than an order of magnitude higher. Lightning spans several kilometers at a voltage in excess of 100 million volts, and one discharge is an arc and a shock wave; across the membrane there is only 0.1-odd volts over a dozen or so atoms, the oil layer does not ionize, and charge can pass only one by one through the holes in proteins. Only when the membrane voltage is pushed artificially to about 0.5–1 V is the membrane broken down into nanometer pores, and when the voltage is removed the pores close again on their own. Laboratories use this to send DNA into cells, which is called electroporation.

The inner membrane is very dense; protons cannot leak back, and only one opening is left.

The Rotor

The opening is ATP synthase. Its lower half, F₀, is embedded in the membrane, a ring of proteins, which in mammals has 8 subunits; its upper half, F₁, extends into the matrix, a three-lobed head; between them a γ shaft connects the two halves.

One H⁺ enters F₀ from the intermembrane space down the drop, and the ring turns one notch. Eight H⁺ come in, the ring makes one full turn, and the γ shaft turns one turn with it. The shaft is bent; with every 120° turn, the three lobes of F₁ take turns changing shape: one lobe opens to take in ADP and Pᵢ, one clamps down so that the two combine into ATP, and one props open to release the finished ATP. In one turn each of the three lobes has its round, making three ATP. Worked out, about 2.7 H⁺ yield one ATP, and with the share for carrying phosphate in, each NADH comes to about 2.5 ATP.

From start to finish one glucose yields about 30–32 ATP, capturing a little over thirty percent of the total energy. The remaining sixty-odd percent becomes heat on the spot, and body temperature is maintained by it.

Open a leak besides the opening and the drop does not pass through the rotor but turns directly into heat. On the inner mitochondrial membrane of brown fat such leaks are specially installed, called UCP1, which newborns use to produce heat in cold weather. In the 1930s someone in the United States sold a chemical called dinitrophenol (DNP) as a diet drug; it ferries protons directly back into the matrix, the rotor spins idle, fat still burns, and the energy all becomes heat; people who took too much lost control of body temperature and some died, and it was banned a few years later.

This rotor can be taken apart and installed elsewhere. In 1974 Racker and Stoeckenius embedded a bacterial light-driven proton pump together with ATP synthase in artificial phospholipid vesicles; under light the pump piled H⁺ into the vesicle and the rotor began to turn and make ATP. This confirmed the account in which a drop is piled up first and a rotor then turns.

The Belt

ATP is adenosine joined to three phosphates. When the outermost phosphate breaks off, ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ, about 30.5 kJ per mole is released under standard conditions; the cell stores much ATP and little ADP, so a break actually releases 50–65 kJ.

ATP is not saved up. At any moment a person has only about 50 g, yet in a day about one body weight's worth is synthesized, broken down and synthesized again, each molecule going round about a thousand times a day. It is a belt: one end hangs on the rotor in the mitochondrion, the other on the uphill reactions all over the body. An uphill reaction cannot run alone; attach to it the breaking of one ATP bond, add the two ΔG, and the sum is negative, and it goes.

Third Drop: Na⁺ Piled Up Outside the Membrane

One end of the belt hangs on the membrane of a neuron.

Embedded in the membrane is the sodium–potassium pump. For every ATP it consumes, it pushes 3 Na⁺ out and pulls 2 K⁺ in, sending out a net positive charge on each turn. The pump never stops, and so the two sides of the membrane are pulled apart: K⁺ is about 140 inside the cell and about 4 mmol/L outside; Na⁺ is the reverse, about 14 inside and about 142 outside. More than half of the chemical energy the brain takes in is spent on these pumps.

Where concentration is uneven, there is voltage. The Nernst equation E = (RT/zF) ln(out/in); at 37 °C RT/F is about 26.7 mV. The equilibrium point of K⁺ is about −95 mV, that of Na⁺ about +62 mV. At rest most of the leak channels open on the membrane are K⁺ channels, the membrane potential lies close to the K⁺ side and rests at about −70 mV.

The phospholipid bilayer is only 4–5 nm thick, with conducting salt water on both sides and a layer of oil in between. This oil layer is a capacitor, about 1 µF per square centimeter. Q = C·V: to move the membrane potential by 100 mV, only about 6,200 monovalent ions per square micrometer of membrane need to be shifted. For a cell body 20 µm in diameter, with a surface of about 1,260 square micrometers, about 8 million Na⁺ enter in one signal; the Na⁺ originally inside the cell is about 35 billion. One pulse moves only about two ten-thousandths of the Na⁺ inside the cell, and the pump has plenty of time to make it up slowly.

A stream of chemical energy has here been split into ions one by one, each carrying 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs.

The Gate

The rim of the bowl is over 50 degrees. The sensory nerve endings in the skin of the fingertip have TRPV1 channels embedded in them, which open above about 43 °C, letting in cations, and the membrane potential at the ending rises.

Lined up on the membrane there are voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, each a single protein molecule with a pore in the middle that only dehydrated Na⁺ can squeeze through. When the membrane potential rises to about −55 mV, the gate of the pore swings open. Ohm's law applies as usual: I = g(V − E). A single channel has a conductance of about 20 pS, and at this moment it is still about 117 mV short of the Na⁺ equilibrium point; one open channel lets in about 15 million Na⁺ per second, and open for about 1 ms, lets in over ten thousand.

The Pulse

As soon as Na⁺ comes in, the membrane potential rises further, and more Na⁺ gates nearby swing open and let in more Na⁺. Positive feedback: within one millisecond the membrane potential surges from −55 to +30 to +40 mV. At the peak the Na⁺ gates jam shut on their own and the K⁺ gates open, K⁺ flows out, and the membrane potential falls back, even a little below −70. For about 1 ms after that the Na⁺ gates cannot open; this is the refractory period, and the pulse can only go forward, not back.

A pulse always has a height of about 100 mV, whether the heat is slight or great. The hotter it is, the more densely the pulses are packed, up to several hundred per second. Intensity is written into frequency.

Pulses run forward along the axon. In a myelinated fiber the pulse is lit anew only at the nodes of Ranvier in the gaps of the myelin, and travels by jumping; the thickest can reach 120 m per second. Heat and pain travel on thin fibers: thinly myelinated Aδ at 5–30 m per second, unmyelinated C fibers at 0.5–2 m. From fingertip to spinal cord is under a meter, and the first to arrive is the prick of pain sent by Aδ.

The Junction

When the pulse reaches the axon terminal, Ca²⁺ gates on the membrane open and Ca²⁺ rushes in, pushing small vesicles full of glutamate up against the membrane, where they burst open. Glutamate, C₅H₉NO₄, a few thousand molecules to a packet, crosses a gap of 20–40 nm and lands on the receptors of the next neuron, opening the gates there, and the membrane potential there rises a little. The whole process takes about 0.5 ms.

Vesicles can only be released whole. The effect released by one junction is the product of three numbers: the number of sites n, the probability of release each time p, and the effect of each vesicle q. n·p·q is the weight of this junction, stored here and used here: the same synapse both remembers this number and multiplies by it at every transmission.

Releasing a vesicle, recycling it and refilling it, plus pumping back the ions that flow in after the downstream receptors open, costs about 1.6 × 10⁵ ATP.

Glutamate is the accelerator. There is another kind of junction that releases GABA, C₄H₉NO₂, opening Cl⁻ gates and pushing the downstream membrane potential down: a brake.

The Node

A neuron has on average from one or two thousand to over three thousand junctions on it, the accelerator and the brake pressed together. Each junction gives only a few tenths of a millivolt to about one millivolt, and they add up on the cell body: those arriving at the same time add, and those arriving one after another add too. If the sum passes −55 mV, a pulse fires; if it does not, silence.

In the spinal cord, this addition passes through only a few levels of interneurons before the motor neurons of the flexor fire, while the extensor side is braked at the same time. The hand pulls back first, and the cortex has not yet "known" that it is hot.

The same train of pulses also goes upward: spinal cord, thalamus, cortex. 86 billion nodes, more than 100 trillion junctions, one layer's firing being the next layer's input. Looking at a picture, one can tell whether there is an animal in it in about 150 ms, only enough for the signal to travel forward once. The whole brain is about 1.4 kg and about 20 W, using about 120 g of glucose a day. Thinking hard all day adds only a few percent to this 20 W. Tiredness is not the electricity bill going up: in a 2022 set of magnetic resonance spectroscopy measurements in Paris, after a day of intensive mental work the glutamate concentration in the lateral prefrontal cortex rose, and people were afterwards more willing to choose the low-effort option. Press the accelerator for long, and the accelerator itself is left piled up there.

The Action

The motor neuron's pulses travel at 50–60 m per second to the muscles of the forearm. The ending releases acetylcholine, C₇H₁₆NO₂⁺, and in under 1 ms the muscle membrane flips. The pulse drives into the interior of the muscle fiber, the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺, Ca²⁺ latches onto troponin C, and the binding sites on the thin filament are uncovered.

The myosin heads on the thick filament grab the thin filament, hydrolyze one ATP, swing once, pull 5–10 nm, let go, and grab again. Thousands upon thousands of heads swing together, the sarcomere shortens, and the finger leaves the rim of the bowl. From touch to withdrawal takes about one to two hundred milliseconds.

The force of the withdrawal was also given by that bowl of rice.

The Rewrite

In the cortex, between the two neurons that fire together at that moment, the junction is changing.

Among the glutamate receptors downstream is one called NMDA, whose pore is normally blocked by a Mg²⁺. Only when glutamate has been released upstream and the downstream side happens to be depolarizing is the Mg²⁺ pushed away and Ca²⁺ let in. The front and the back must differ by no more than about 20 ms, and the two things must happen at the same time. Once Ca²⁺ comes in, it activates CaMKII, more AMPA receptors are inserted into the membrane, and the head of the dendritic spine swells by one to two times. q grows larger, and n may increase too. What fires together is wired more tightly together.

A change like this holds for one to three hours at first. At night, in sleep, the hippocampus replays the day's firing sequences at about ten to twenty times the speed; genes in the nucleus are awakened and synthesize new protein, nailing the temporary change down for days and longer, in some cases for a lifetime.

The next time he lifts that bowl, the fingers stop half a centimeter short of its rim.

The Last Joule

Every level leaks. From sugar to ATP, over sixty percent leaks away; ATP driving pumps and myosin leaks another stretch; ions flowing through gates also turn friction into heat. The energy of that bowl of rice, not a joule short, all ends up as heat.

The whole machine gives off about 80 joules a second. At rest, about sixty percent radiates from the skin, a little over twenty percent leaves with evaporating water, about fifteen percent is handed to the air, and the rest is passed to whatever touches the body. Body temperature stays around 37 °C: when there is too much heat, the hypothalamus makes the skin's blood vessels dilate and the sweat glands sweat; when there is too little, it makes the vessels constrict and the muscles shiver. The energy ledger requires only that heat production equal heat loss; at which degree it stops is decided by the hypothalamic set point.

Over a day: about 360 liters of O₂ breathed in, about 290 liters of CO₂ breathed out, about 7 × 10³ kJ coming in from food and leaving again as heat. About forty kilograms of ATP go through once and return to the few tens of grams they started as. The brain alone pushes out and lets flow back about forty-odd moles of Na⁺ a day, about a kilogram or so.

The pumps, gates, rotors and receptors are all proteins, all assembled according to instructions in which three bases on DNA make one word, and assembling also eats ATP. Even the line that makes the parts runs in the same way.

Pile up, hold back, release, flow through. From the beam of light a rice plant took in to the half centimeter beyond the rim of the bowl.

The Close

When a bowl of rice has run its course, the whole machine has used only one way of running. About 30 trillion cells, each nucleated cell carrying a diploid set of DNA of about 6.2 billion base pairs; it grows itself, lights up a brain with about twenty watts, and runs for seventy or eighty years on rice and water. In assembly density, energy efficiency and self-repair, humanity has so far been unable to build a second one.

This machine leaks too. The proton drop across the inner mitochondrial membrane is about 0.2 V; in most tissues, the older one gets, the lower the dam, and the fewer ATP the same bowl of rice can push. Experiments to restore the drop already exist: in worms fitted with a light-controlled proton pump, raising the inner membrane potential back lengthened lifespan; in older people, twelve weeks of high-intensity interval training raised the output of muscle mitochondria by about 69%. If the light-controlled kind were to be moved to people, the retina would rank first: it has the highest energy consumption in the body, and light can reach it.

Building it costs not a cent; reading it costs very little. In 2024 Harvard and Google sliced one cubic millimeter of human cortex into 5,019 sections and reconstructed about 57,000 cells and 150 million synapses, with 1.4 petabytes of data. A brain is about 1.2 million cubic millimeters, and what has been read is only this one grain of rice in size. The US BRAIN Initiative's 2025 funding of 321 million US dollars is about one ten-thousandth of humanity's total annual R&D spending (about 3.07 trillion US dollars).

Everyone carries the most sophisticated machine known; the one grain of rice that has been read is the fullest page of its manual to date.

Section 32 · Hunger, Malnutrition and Obesity

Two Opposite Nutritional Imbalances

Of children under five, 150.2 million do not grow to the height they should, and another 35.5 million are already overweight.

On the same planet, two opposite nutritional imbalances exist at once: not eating enough (hunger, stunting, wasting) and eating too much (overweight, obesity). How many people are on each side.

Hunger is dietary energy (kcal/day) intake that stays below the amount needed to sustain basal metabolism and activity, with supply chronically insufficient and intermittent. To keep a carbon-based vehicle running, what is missing is more than kilocalories: the three fuels of carbohydrate, fat and protein, water H₂O, and micronutrients such as iron Fe, iodine I, zinc Zn and vitamin A — lacking any one will not do. Enough energy but too few micronutrients is called hidden hunger. Eating too much is intake that stays above expenditure, the excess stored in adipose tissue in the form of triglycerides.

The body's fuel tanks have sizes: glycogen about 0.2 kg, about 900 kcal, enough for only half a day to a day; triglycerides in adipose tissue about 15 kg, about 1.41 × 10⁵ kcal, enough for one to two months; mobilizable protein about 6 kg (Cahill 1970, NEJM). The safe intake of dietary protein is about 0.83 g/(kg·day) (WHO/FAO/UNU 2007).

Against the Supply Ledger

Calculated earlier: the dietary energy supply per person is 2,978 kcal/day, above the average requirement per person. The next section: each year about 1.052 billion tons of food is wasted at retail, in food service and in households.

RatioCalculationResultGrade
Waste ÷ hungry population1.052 billion tons ÷ 673 million peopleAbout 1.56 tons per hungry person per year, 4.3 kg a dayB

This does not mean that shipping the wasted food to the hungry would solve hunger — the wasted food is in the refrigerators of homes in rich cities, while hunger is concentrated in conflict zones and poor rural areas. This row only puts two ledgers side by side to see the orders of magnitude: hunger is not a problem of total quantity.
