What This Ledger Shows (Only What a Ledger Can Show)

  1. The total is not lacking; the distribution is. Per-capita calorie supply is higher than need, yet 673 million people are still hungry; food waste is 1.052 billion tons.
  2. The costliest leaks in life are at the two ends: chronic disease under age five and before age 70 together lose about 780 million life-years every year.
  3. Half of labor has no price: unpaid care makes up about 43% of all human labor, and 76% of it is borne by women.
  4. The great majority of civilization's time has not been invested in "finding out what is not yet known": research takes about 3.6 in 10,000 of all human time.
  5. The population's uphill climb has sixty years left, yet the slope of aging has already begun: the old-age dependency ratio will rise from 16 to 40 by 2100.

The Largest Number in This Ledger Was Made

The largest quantity counted in the whole ledger is neither the 106 billion tons of matter nor the $118.2 trillion. It is time:

The second row is more than half of this cohort's expected lifetime total (about 597 billion person-years, 8.162 billion × 73.17). In 1900, life expectancy at birth for all humanity was 32.0 years, and in 2023 it was 73.17 years; those additional 41.17 years multiplied by 8.162 billion people is the second row.

For those alive today, more than half of the time in a lifetime was not inborn; it was learned.

The third row is the cost: five thousand years of accumulated classroom time is less than half of the first row. The fourth row is this generation's continued investment: 11.9 million person-years a year, 3.6 in 10,000 of humanity's person-years.

These four rows are what this ledger finally has to say. Every second, every ton and every dollar counted over the preceding hundred-odd sections falls into the balance in the first row; and since that balance has an origin, it can be made larger, and it can be made smaller. Volumes 19 and 20 work out how to make it larger, and how to divide it once it is larger.

All four rows are Grade C, and the four weak points are written into the four loopholes in the section on life extension; bring them along when citing.

Closing Note: The Empty Cells (★ My Position)

Across the whole ledger, 300 places are marked as missing numbers (Appendix Table D): 144 Unverified and 156 Uncounted. For the Unverified, the numbers have already been counted by someone, scattered through national statistics bureaus, industry associations and reports of various years, but the master ledger has not yet pieced them into one global table: employment by occupation, grain use by type, the number of banks in the world, the size of the sports industry. For the Uncounted, no institution in the world has ever counted: a broiler goes through six links from hatching egg to table, and four of the middle links are not counted; how many near-collisions precede a road accident is not counted; how many relatives are left bearing grief after a person dies is not counted; how many thieves, how many sex workers and how many people live by divination in the whole world is not counted. This is not an omission in statistics; it is a place that statistics have never reached.

What these 300 empty cells show is not the failure of any one institution, but that humanity still has no ledger covering all its members that is real-time, trustworthy and shared. Wherever numbers are missing, humanity urgently needs to build an interstellar civilization blockchain network — once that network stands, every birth, every diagnosis, every work hour, every ton of matter and every departure is entered into the same tamper-proof public ledger; by then every table in this Master Ledger of Human Civilization can be updated by the second.

The most unusual thing about this ledger is that the further it goes, the less it looks like a ledger.

The first eighteen volumes record what has already happened, with sources, years and grades. Volumes 19 and 20 record what has not yet happened — those cells are still empty today.

Section 121 · Back to One Person's Day

Giving the Average Back to a Specific Person

This person does not exist — he is the average of 8.162 billion people. But every number in his day can be traced to a source earlier in the book.

His Day

Midnight to eight. He sleeps 8 hours (C). In those 8 hours, about 121,000 children are born in the world and about 57,000 people leave.

Morning. Behind the food he eats is the production layer's roughly 1 kilogram of grain, 330 grams of milk and 125 grams of meat per person per day. This food is produced by 928 million agricultural workers — he alone "uses up" 41 minutes of their day. He drinks about 2.5 liters of water; civilization draws about 1,300 liters for him.

Late morning to afternoon. If he is employed, he works about 6 hours (42 hours a week spread over each day); if he is the average of the whole population, he works only 2.6 hours, and another 2 hours go to housework and caring for family. One work hour creates on average about $15 of GDP, and he receives about $7.9. He spends 78 minutes on the road.

Evening. If he is an internet user, he is online about 6.5 hours, of which 2 hours 21 minutes are on social media. He generates about 4 GB of network traffic. His day is 2 hours 21 minutes of the whole of humanity's social media time; and spread over each person, all of humanity's research time comes to only about 31 seconds a day, calculated as 3.6 in 10,000 × 24 hours.

The part that cannot be seen. On this day, civilization burns 55.9 kilowatt-hours of primary energy for him, a continuous power of about 2,330 watts — about the heat output of 23 human bodies; it emits about 12 kilograms of energy-related CO₂ for him; digs up about 35.6 kilograms of matter for him; and produces about 0.7 kilograms of municipal waste for him.

One Person's Life

73.3 years, 26,772 days, 2.313 billion seconds. About 24 years of a life are spent asleep; if he works continuously from age 20 to 60, about 84,000 hours; over a lifetime he is "allotted" about 34 tons of cement, 17 tons of steel, 28 tons of grain, 705 chickens, 221 pairs of shoes, and about 950 tons of extracted matter.

Of the 8.162 billion present today, he is part of the 7% still alive among the roughly 117 billion ever born. By 2100, he and almost all of his generation will have left, and the next cohort of about 9.5 billion will go on keeping this ledger.

This person does not exist.

Every number in the preceding hundred-odd sections is what remains once 8.16 billion people have been flattened out. In the course of flattening, mothers who died in difficult childbirth and people who lived to a hundred were added together, 670 million people going hungry and kitchens that throw away a billion tons of food a year were added together, and 2.2 billion people who are not online and people who scroll for 2 hours 21 minutes a day were added together.

So the person this ledger finally presents is the only thing in the whole ledger that does not exist.

But the numbers corresponding to each person all exist, and are happening at this very moment.

The ledger ends here. The line for the next second is still empty.
