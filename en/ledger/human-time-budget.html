Section 10 · The Time Pool of 8 Billion People: 195.9 Billion Person-Hours a Day

Adding up the day of 8 billion people

Add together the day of every person in all of humanity, and you get civilization's daily time pool. How big the pool is, and the destinations that can be checked, cut into blocks: sleep, work counted in GDP, unpaid care, travel, going online, social media, research.

Boundary: the destinations of time overlap: going online can happen while working, care can happen while traveling. The cuts here are "blocks measured separately on different bases," not a mutually exclusive pie. A mutually exclusive pie would require a globally uniform time-use survey, which does not currently exist (no statistics).

The third ratio is a figure to which the second half of the ledger returns again and again. It does not say there are too few researchers (11.9 million full-time equivalents is already the most in human history), but that the great majority of civilization's time pool has never touched the work of "turning the unknown into the known."

What to look at are Link 5 and Link 6.

Time spent scrolling social media

The 24 hours of a day are the 24 hours within the 195.9 billion person-hours of this day of civilization. Internet users spend an average of 2 hours 21 minutes a day on social media, and this 2 hours 21 minutes multiplied by 5.79 billion is 13.6 billion person-hours: enough to keep all the world's researchers working for about 190 days.

Where the figures are unreliable

  • Sleep, going online, and social media are Grade C basis estimates, and overlap one another.
  • Unpaid care of 16.4 billion hours is an ILO 2018 estimate (extrapolated from time-use surveys in 64 countries); the year is old and no update exists for now.
  • The 11.9 million researchers is a UIS extrapolation from density per million population, Grade B.

The pool itself is also made

What this section counts is a flow: 195.9 billion person-hours a day, 71.5 trillion hours a year.

The size of this pool depends on two things: how many people, and how long each can live. The second has more than doubled in the past one hundred twenty-three years (32.0 → 73.17 years).

IfDaily person-hoursRatio to today
Today (8.162 billion people, life expectancy 73.17 years)195.9 billion1.00
Population unchanged, life expectancy back to 1900's 32.0 yearsThe pool's stock (sum of remaining life) would be smaller by about 336 billion person-years—

Note the distinction here: the daily flow looks only at how many people are alive today, not at how long they can live; lifespan affects the stock (358.5 billion person-years). So the statement "technology has more than doubled the time pool" speaks of the stock pool, not of the daily flow.

The two pools must be recorded separately; talking about them mixed would turn a real conclusion into an exaggeration. The section at the end of the ledger that computes life extension uses the stock basis.

An employed woman's total labor time each day is about 1.9 hours more than an employed man's. That is about 690 hours more a year, equivalent to 16 more weeks of work at 42 hours a week (assembled on the bases above, C).

Another account: worldwide, 708 million women are outside the labor force because of unpaid care (ILO 2024, A), more than the entire population of Latin America and the Caribbean (663 million). Under the 2018 basis this figure was 606 million.

One finding of Gethin and Saez: as countries grow richer, the hours of the young and the old fall (longer schooling, wider pension coverage), while the hours of ages 20–59 are remarkably stable over the long run: the decline in men's hours is exactly offset by the rise in women's employment.

The curve is an inverted U: from poor to middle income, hours rise slightly (more people enter wage labor); from middle income to rich, hours fall (working-hour regulation, paid leave, expansion of the formal sector).

Screens: the new largest block

DataReportal (2026): internet users aged 16–64 spend on average 6 hours 38 minutes a day online, of which social media takes 2 hours 21 minutes; on another basis, internet users spend about 18 hours 36 minutes a week on social and video feeds, about 16% of waking time.

Going online overlaps with work, study, and commuting, and cannot be subtracted from the 24 hours. But it has a clear meaning: for an urban internet user, the screen is already the largest block of time apart from sleep.

A whole-population average day has 8 hours of sleep (C), 2.6 hours of paid work (B), 2.0 hours of care (B), 1.3 hours of travel (B), and a remainder of 10.1 hours (C). The problem lies in the allocation of Link 3: 76.2% of care labor is borne by women, the invisible half of the labor accounts.

Where the figures are unreliable

  • There is no uniform global time-use survey; ILO 2018 is extrapolated from 64 countries (covering about two thirds of the world's working-age population).
  • Sleep is a basis (C). Global totals of study time, mealtime, and religious activity time are unverified.
  • The daily hours of the employed are spread over 7 days, without deducting holidays; Gethin and Saez have made holiday corrections for some countries.
