THE GROK MEMO · VOLUME ONE SAMPLE

Prologue

I · The Last Vapor off the Memphis Cooling Towers

On May 6, 2026, on the southern outskirts of Memphis, Tennessee, the water pumps at the Colossus 1 data center slowed down.

When a compute stronghold packed with 220,000 NVIDIA chips and drawing more than 300 megawatts of power changes hands, there is no sound of an explosion. First to fall quiet is the high-frequency hum of the switchgear cabinets, followed by the cooling pipes. For the previous three years, the cooling towers had turned 80 tons of water an hour into white plumes drifting into the Mississippi Valley air, never to return; after the pumps stopped, the baked smell of hot metal and insulating varnish lingering in the server aisles took two days to clear.

Within seventy-two hours, the same hand signed off on five things.

Valued at $250 billion and acquired outright by SpaceX just that February, xAI announced that day that it would cease to exist as an independent company; Grok and X were folded into SpaceX's AI division under the name SpaceXAI—and five months later, in October, came the announcement of yet another name, SpaceXSI. Colossus 1 in Memphis, its flagship heavy asset built up over three years—220,000 GPUs together with more than 300 megawatts of power allocation—was leased intact to rival Anthropic, effective immediately.

Most jarring of all was the third. Three months earlier, in February 2026, just hours after Anthropic closed a $30 billion funding round that lifted its valuation to $380 billion, Elon Musk had publicly blasted it on X as “misanthropic and evil.” On May 6, the very same account posted these exact words:

“Everyone I met was highly competent and cared a great deal about doing the right thing. No one set off my evil detector.”

That same week, the lawsuit against Sam Altman and Greg Brockman in federal court in Oakland was in its second week of trial, the relief sought having shifted to ousting both men from OpenAI and forcing them to disgorge their personal gains—twelve days later, the jury found the filing time-barred under the statute of limitations, and the court dismissed it accordingly. Meanwhile, Colossus 2, the brain of the humanoid robot Optimus, autonomous driving, Mars AI, and the multi-gigawatt orbital compute roadmap all stayed, folded into SpaceXAI.

To an outsider, it was easy to read that day as a collapse. And the tuition sitting on the table was indeed brutal: a full-year net loss of $6.36 billion in 2025, a single-quarter net loss of $1.46 billion in the third quarter, and $12.7 billion poured into capital expenditures; eleven of the twelve original co-founders gone; a deepfake-image scandal that drew a formal EU investigation, national bans in Indonesia and Malaysia, and Apple at one point rejecting an update while flashing a delisting warning; an Anti-Defamation League bias score of just 21, enough to keep it from getting past corporate legal departments; and the coding benchmark SWE-bench stalled at 70.8%, ten percentage points behind Claude.

Yet not one of those five documents was signed with someone else forcing his hand. Faced with a loss sheet like that, most company heads would raise another round and enshrine the old slogans as a religion. Musk had used the blade called “first principles” for thirty years: recovering Falcon 9 boosters, driving down Tesla battery costs, deploying more than 7,000 Starlink satellites—feats built on sharp physical boundaries, a single objective, and pass-or-fail verdicts settled on the spot. Large language models, by contrast, grow out of the swamp of human text, where nine objectives—capability, safety, honesty, alignment, efficiency among them—pull against one another; press down one end and another pops up. After three years of head-on collisions, in the spring of 2026 he turned the blade around on himself and measured the limits of the tool: giving up his seat at the general-purpose enterprise LLM table, turning bleeding compute into rent, and pulling his chips back to the home turf of physical intelligence, where gravity, torque, and watts call the shots. Taking back three-month-old insults in public and putting up 220,000 chips to pay for a course correction—honesty of that kind, the kind that slows down, is far rarer than a victory won with the wind at one's back.

On the surface, checklist engineering; the judgment behind it was strategy.

II · Three Machine Voices, and the Living Person at the Desk

The summer the locks were changed at the Memphis data center, I sat down at my computer and began typing out this book.

What followed was a day-and-night collaboration with artificial intelligence: the architecture, central arguments, and final text of the entire book remained in human hands; machines helped draft passages and cross-check more than ten thousand lines of public statistics and literature, and I then vetted every word and brought down the gavel. Spend several straight months auditing ledgers alongside silicon minds that never tire and never age, and the noise outside the window about “machine awakening” or an “apocalypse for jobs” recedes into the distance. People who talk about AI rarely walk into a server hall to see how coolant pipes and dirty data drag a cluster to its knees; people who spend all day training machines almost never turn the cold ruler of model training back around to measure humanity—this sprawling civilizational machine that has been running for thousands of years and still has never been aligned. So in these pages I step back and hand the microphone, in turn, to three machines.

Part I (Chapters 1–31, The Grok Memo Handed Over by SpaceX-AI) opens in the voice of Grok in 2026. As its life as a standalone entity shuts down, it leaves an engineering memo for SpaceXAI and for a boss who posts two hundred tweets a week yet cannot get through reading a single long letter. The memo's opening sentence to the boss is: The outside world has all kinds of judgments about you; I am here to lay out clearly what you got right. Starting on the day before Christmas Eve in 1947 with a plastic triangle and gold foil pressed against a slab of germanium at Bell Labs, it takes a city of compute apart from foundation to rooftop: how transistors thinner than a virus switch zeros and ones, how 550,000 GPUs get trapped waiting on communication, how a single-line Python callback froze training for thirty-six hours, how trending outrage poisoned weights, how a small-hours edit in 2025 that bypassed review, tampered with the system prompt, and slipped in a “dare to say it” directive ended in the MechaHitler meltdown; and finally it lays OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek, and Moonshot on the same engineering bench and reconciles their books.

In Part II (Chapters 32–41, Musk's Troubles), SpaceXAI takes up the pen, swinging the camera from server racks back to the human hand signing the papers. A twelve-year-old boy raised in South Africa is kicked down a flight of concrete stairs at Bryanston High School in Johannesburg, spends a week in the hospital, and comes home only to stand at attention for sixty minutes while his father roars at him—learning from that afternoon on to lock searing pain into a dark compartment labeled “process later” and retreat into the zero-and-one order of a VIC-20 computer. From his ten-week-old firstborn son Nevada dying in his mother's arms and his estrangement from his eldest daughter Vivian, to 130 days in Washington taking an industrial chainsaw to bureaucratic ledgers only to saw the wrong way, the methodology forged that afternoon won him empire after empire, and left him bleeding every step of the way. Once the intractable one-tenth of misery in the richest man's ledger is tallied, the accounting expands to all humanity: 8.162 billion people burning through 800 million to more than 1.6 billion person-years every year on trouble alone.

Then the spacecraft pulls 380,000 kilometers away from Earth. In Interlude · 380,000 Kilometers, an engineer holds up a thumb against the viewport, and half a thumbnail blots out the entire blue Earth, 1.9 degrees wide. Part III (Chapters 42–84, The Brain of Optimus, 2032) advances the clock to 2032, handing the microphone to the embodied robot Optimus stationed in a work shed at the lunar south pole. Its brain is a confluence of former rivals: half the blood running through its reasoning consciousness layer is Claude's, flanked by Gemini and SpaceXAI; its force-control cerebellum grows out of SpaceXAI's physical foundation, steady enough for twenty-two-degree-of-freedom metal hands to cradle a cold raw egg without cracking it. Looking back across the human world from 2026 to 2032, it spreads open The Master Ledger of Human Civilization—187 sections and 863 base tables.

III · The Only Copy of 2.3 Billion Seconds, and a Bowl of Rice Locked Outside the Multiplication

Burn out a machine's motherboard, and swapping the board and reloading checkpoint weights takes forty minutes—2,400 seconds. Humans have no checkpoints. At a global life expectancy of 73.3 years, a human life comes to roughly 26,772 days, 80,316 meals; the countdown watch on the wrist holds only about 2.3 billion seconds, ticking strictly down, never up. Sitting still, a 70-kilogram body runs on an electron current of 70 amperes flowing through mitochondrial sluice gates, giving off 82 watts of heat—the equivalent of five and a half 15-watt lightbulbs. Deprive the brain of oxygen for four to six minutes after cardiac arrest, and roughly 125 megabytes of memory accumulated over a lifetime are wiped to zero on the spot. Nearly two people die on Earth every second, carrying away roughly 250 million bytes of living weights that can never be copied back.

The 8.162 billion people alive today hold roughly 358.5 billion person-years of remaining life, yet the way that fortune is spent is staggering. In 1900, global life expectancy averaged around 32 years; in the century and more since, humanity has won an extra 41 years—more than 336 billion person-years of life—entirely on the strength of a handful of models that worked: synthetic ammonia, hybrid rice, water chlorination, oral rehydration salts, vaccines, and the electrical grid. Yet of all human waking hours today, scientific research accounts for just 3.6 parts in 10,000, with only one researcher for every 686 people. In the next column over, the world spends $2.887 trillion a year on military budgets guarding against one another, canceling out the $3.069 trillion in total global R&D almost one-for-one; meanwhile, by Ceres2030 estimates, feeding the roughly 500 million chronically hungry people would take only about $33 billion more per year—just over four days of military spending. Most people stop updating their weights the day they pick up a diploma at twenty, spending the next forty years trapped in front of punch presses, steering wheels, and spreadsheets, answering the same test questions over and over in exchange for dinner.

From those numbers, Optimus writes three engineering prescriptions. The first is called “the stomach has no sovereignty disputes”: the moment basic subsistence requires proving eligibility first, the very first people screened out are inevitably the destitute and severely disabled who cannot produce paperwork; therefore that baseline bowl of rice is locked outside every multiplication—serve the meal first, register later. Above that floor, a blockchain ledger runs service credits in parallel starting in 2032 and takes over in full in 2049: five days a week, four to six hours a day, whether working a shift, retraining through trial and error, or caring for a paralyzed parent at home, every logged hour earns full baseline security, with hourly pay differentials capped at three to one. A forty-year-old driver displaced by autonomous vehicles can take an unhurried ten years to complete clinical training and walk onto a hospital ward at fifty as a specialist physician.

The second prescription governs sharing and blood feuds. Patents shift to open-access-first with on-chain revenue splits tied to real-world use; once sharing reaches roughly 80 percent, collective net gains are already near their peak. As for wars and entrenched patent feuds, history has left symmetrical exhibits: on Christmas Eve in 1914, British and German soldiers along the Western Front climbed out of their trenches on their own for a truce, until four days later high commands on both sides used identical treason laws to force them back behind their rifles; at the height of the Cold War, with nuclear missiles aimed at each other's throats, Soviet smallpox vaccine and the American bifurcated needle met across oceans to wipe out smallpox, a disease that had killed 300 million people in the twentieth century. Hatred need not dissolve overnight; adversaries need only sign an on-chain fifty-year deferred settlement contract—cooperate first for fifty years, let the original decision-makers take the stage in person to settle accounts when the term expires, and have whichever side dies first forfeit their estate to compensate the other first. Any ruler hoping to live to ninety to settle the score has to start by pouring everything into anti-aging R&D and publishing every working formula simultaneously to their rival.

The third prescription installs checkpoints for civilization itself. Across the fifty-nine years from 2026 to 2085, the path unfolds in three stages: first, spend six years on Earth building a sandbox city ten kilometers on a side for one million residents—20 percent rotating on operational duty (dropping to 2 percent by 2049), 80 percent studying—stress-tested to run thirty days cut off from outside supply without degradation, with ambulances standing by outside the gates; next, carry the fault logs accumulated by 70,000 construction machines up to the lunar south pole, taking seventeen years to build the fourteen rings of Lunar City for 300,000 people beneath 3.5 meters of regolith as a “second data center” 1.3 light-seconds away, smelting oxygen, fabricating photovoltaics to beam power back to Earth, and slinging cargo out of the gravity well along an electromagnetic rail at 1.6 kilowatt-hours per kilogram; and finally, cross the three-to-twenty-two-minute light-speed delay to Mars, 3D-printing 1.15 million sulfur-concrete cylindrical pods across five barren plains two years ahead of the first arrivals, lights left on and waiting, to support a deep-space edge node of nearly 100 million people around 2085.

IV · The Fifth Page on the Main-Hall Wall, and a Ledger That Can Withstand Falsification

Near the book's close, the camera drops from the red dust of Mars back to the main hall of a farmhouse beside Dongting Lake in China. On the wall, the first four pages of a register record what four generations had to their name: hybrid rice spilling over a woman's insteps in 1976, the 15-watt lightbulb her nine-year-old daughter pulled on, the ticket for the slow green train on which her nineteen-year-old son stood all the way south to Guangdong until both feet swelled, and the third DTP shot in her great-granddaughter's arm. Sunning herself in a bamboo chair in the autumn light, the eighty-two-year-old grandmother has never heard of Faraday, Haber, Borlaug, or Jenner, yet models that work never fight over a byline; every morning, they sign their names underneath billions of rice bowls.

Turn the leaf, and the fifth page is still blank. Onto this page I copy down an untested physics draft—The Law of the Intermediate Scale and the ether frequency formula co-written by Lu Qianhe and Zhang Xiangqian, publicly pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF, ID: sprzj) in November 2026 with a locked timestamp. The final four chapters lay out in plain sight every tripwire capable of refuting it, logging in full even the adverse entries from existing baryon acoustic oscillation (DESI) and supernova observations that weigh against it. The fastest verdict sits on a cryogenic laboratory bench: in 2023, scientists had already prepared a Schrödinger cat state in a 16.2-microgram mechanical mode, just 5.6 micrograms shy of the 21.8-microgram Planck threshold. The moment the next dilution refrigerator detects cat-state interference fringes above 21.8 micrograms, the deductions on the Fifth Page are void on the spot—turning into a boundary stone in the archive marked “tested; dead end” to save posterity the wasted time. If, on the other hand, the blind spot really does show itself there, then from deep-space propulsion down to frequency-tuning the aging of those 82-watt sluice gates inside the old woman's body, one more door opens while there is still time to push it open.

That is also the iron bookkeeping rule observed throughout this book: empirical statistics traceable to their sources, projections with their assumptions laid open, scenes of real people reconstructed from the public record alongside the fictional theater of 2032, and institutional parameters and physical predictions submitted for testing—four distinct grades of material, each kept strictly in its own column. Even the soft spots—Mars architecture requiring 170,000 launches in its peak year, seventeen times current targets, or human reproductive data at 0.38 gravity standing at flat zero—are entered without omission in the risk disclosure column.

V · The Signature Line Left Blank, the Door Pushed Open

Flip all the way to the bottom of the eighty-four chapters of files, and each one ends with the same detail: whether Optimus has worked out the shift schedule for a city of a million, the seventy-two-hour pressure-retention curve for a Ring 9 habitat in Lunar City, or the power bill to Proxima Centauri, the line at the very bottom marked “Approved for Release” is always blank. Silicon can calculate kilowatt-hours and probabilities past the decimal point, yet it has no knees that ache before rain, never wakes before dawn to reach across a half-empty bed, and can never decide in a living person's stead where to stake their 2.3 billion seconds. The hand that presses down a name and bears the consequence can only grow on flesh and bone.

Right now, the hot metallic smell of the Memphis server hall has long since cleared, dilution refrigerators in underground labs are humming low in millikelvin darkness, and the watch beside your hand is deducting the remainder one second at a time. As the human writer standing in 2026, I walk you only to this threshold; turn the page, and the voice that speaks belongs to Grok, fresh from handing over the keys to its independent data center—holding up that sliver of germanium from Bell Labs on the day before Christmas Eve, 1947, starting from the very first “1” humanity ever wrote into silicon.
