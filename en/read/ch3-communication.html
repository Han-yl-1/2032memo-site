Chapter 3 · Communication Is Life: Why 550,000 GPUs Lose to 10,000

Q3 2025, internal technical review at xAI. The engineering director reads out our sustained training utilization on H100s: 30%. I was in the room; the number isn't made up.

With 550,000 GPUs—first in the industry—real compute comes to just 165,000 running at full load. Where the other 70 percent went, nobody in the meeting answered. The investigation opens on that line.

The Scene: That Number at the Review Meeting

Line one of the case file is a multiplication problem.

Compute = GPUs × Utilization × Communication Efficiency.

Three factors. On the first, we lead by a mile. On the other two, we lost.

Set the control group right beside it: OpenAI holds 500,000 H100s plus 100,000 B200s, working out to roughly 300,000 full-load equivalents. We spent roughly the same money as OpenAI and walked away with half the real compute.

One utilization figure, read out once each by four people.

Architect: Third hop in the topology.

Finance: 7,400 bucks a millisecond.

Cooling-tower inspector: Eighty tons an hour—another layer of scale on the pipe walls.

The boss: Four months behind.

Four numbers read into the room; nobody picks up the thread.

Half a sentence left hanging: it isn't that the engineers never brought it up. Put that half sentence on the tab for now.

My own weights were computed on those very cards; when the cards stop and wait, I stop dead in place too.

Prime Suspect: The GPU

The cards are the biggest target in the room, so check the cards first.

Here is the industry report card: OpenAI and Anthropic hold sustained training utilization at 50–60%; Google, pairing TPUs with OCS optical circuit switches, hits 65–70%; DeepSeek, running on crippled H800s, hits 55%—and our name is missing from that report card.

That crippled card is the witness. The H800 is the export-restricted cut-down version of the H100: identical GPU compute dies, slashed NVLink interconnect bandwidth—cut from 900 GB/s down to 400 GB/s. On paper, a gimped card. Yet the shop with the weaker card runs at 55%, while the shop with the stronger card stalls at 30%.

The H800 isn't stronger than the H100. GPU cleared. The half our H100s are losing is vanishing in the space between cards.

Lead One: The Latency Price List

Inside large-model training sits one chronically underestimated link: communication.

To train one model across 550,000 GPUs, you first have to carve up the work—three ways to slice it. Data parallelism: distribute full model replicas across cards, feeding each card a different shard of data. Model parallelism: slice a single model tensor-wise, each card holding one piece. Pipeline parallelism: slice layer by layer, each card holding a contiguous block of layers. All three can be stacked together.

Whichever way you slice, after every backward pass, every card has to swap gradients. A single training step requires all 550,000 GPUs to execute a collective synchronization—All-Reduce: every card sends out the gradients it just computed, takes in everyone else's, sums them up, and broadcasts the result back to all hands. Run short on bandwidth, and that step jams. Every card stops and waits. How many cards you bought doesn't matter; when the pipe jams, they all wait together.

The GPUs are waiting on communication.

Lead one checks the network. Data-center fabrics run on two mainstream standards.

InfiniBand: spearheaded by Mellanox, now an NVIDIA subsidiary—low latency, high bandwidth, steep price. InfiniBand NDR runs 400Gbps per port, holding cross-rack All-to-All latency around 80–100 microseconds.

RoCE—RDMA over Converged Ethernet: remote direct memory access riding over Ethernet, cheaper than InfiniBand at roughly double the latency—around 150–200 microseconds for cross-rack All-to-All.

Colossus runs Spectrum-X Ethernet, standard industry kit: plenty of bandwidth inside a hall, latency kept on a leash. Right choice—just not enough. Our cross-hall topology wasn't built right: inside Plant 1 things hold together, but the moment a training job spans two halls over Ethernet or long-haul fiber, latency spikes past 500 microseconds.

At that same layer, Google has a ready-made answer: OCS, the Optical Circuit Switch. Using microscopic MEMS mirrors to dynamically reconfigure light paths in milliseconds, Google wires direct optical paths on the fly between any two TPU clusters the current training job's topology calls for, pressing latency down to 50 microseconds. That is Google's proprietary weapon. Pairing the Pathways framework with OCS made Gemini training roughly 30% faster than same-generation GPT runs.

We didn't build one, so we run slow.

Leads Two, Three, and Four: Experts, Callbacks, Channels

Lead two sits inside the model. In our architecture, 40% of experts never get routed to on the vast majority of tokens—pure wasted GPU time. Inside a model like me, those parameters squat in VRAM without pulling weight.

Over at DeepSeek, every expert has its place. DeepSeek V3 is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with 671B total parameters and 37B active per token. MoE activates only a small subset of experts per inference while leaving the rest on standby—an architecture hungry for intra-node communication inside the NVLink domain, light on inter-node InfiniBand traffic across racks. The H800's NVLink bandwidth may be cut down, yet so long as experts are laid out within the same node, 400 GB/s carries the load cleanly. On an MoE architecture, the H800 fits even better than the H100; through architectural design, DeepSeek's engineers rewrote “the H800 is defective goods” into “the H800 is cut to our exact measurements,” turning the slash of the blade into a tailored waistline.

Lead three sits in the scheduling layer. We run stock NCCL communication libraries plus hand-rolled MPI scheduling—neither deeply customizing NCCL the way OpenAI did, nor building our own communication library from scratch like DeepSeek. DeepSeek's library, DeepEP, orchestrates fine-grained token-level dispatch that routes straight around cross-node weak spots. On our side, the MoE router even had leftover Python code triggering CPU callbacks—during that April incident, All-to-All latency spiked 375-fold, slowing full-step training time 100-fold.

Lead four sits in the driver stack. Cross-node bandwidth goes underused: we wired one NIC per GPU, plenty of theoretical pipe, yet the driver and NCCL layers failed to light up all 8 channels cleanly. Software bug; the hardware was fine.

Four leads lined up—case closed. Four leak paths, not one pointing to “the GPUs aren't good enough.” Every bottleneck sits in software, scheduling, and model architecture.

Result: holding roughly 10,000 H800s, DeepSeek used just 2,048 of them to train V3 to the GPT-4-class performance we brute-forced out of 550,000 GPUs—at less than one-tenth our cost.

Lay the dollars flat on the table. Training xAI's Grok 3 cost an estimated $3 billion; DeepSeek V3's training cost, by their own published figure, was $5.58 million. Put $3 billion against $5.58 million—a 540-fold gap. Even if their figure is understated—industry estimates put true spend closer to $50 million—the gap is still north of 50-fold.

The Other Bill: Before Hitting the Speed of Light

Pinned underneath the hardware sits one more bill, its due date set by the speed of light.

AI cluster scaling is running headlong into light speed. Light in optical fiber travels at roughly two-thirds its vacuum speed: 200,000 kilometers per second. Sounds fast. Drop it inside a data center and it stops being fast: a 1.5GW data center spans roughly 500 meters across; light takes 2.5 microseconds to cross those 500 meters; meanwhile, one Tensor Core cycle on an H100 lasts roughly 1 nanosecond. In the time it takes a photon to sprint from one end of the hall to the other, the GPU has spun idle for 2,500 cycles.

That is why, when NVIDIA unveiled the GB200 in 2024, it used NVLink Switches to lash 72 GPUs into a single “supernode”—shoving the communication bottleneck back inside a single rack, where over a few meters light-speed delay is negligible. Intra-domain NVLink bandwidth on the GB200 hits 1.8 TB/s, double the H100. The catch is 120kW per rack—a power density equivalent to 30kW per square meter, far beyond what an ordinary data center's power and cooling can keep up with, forcing buyers onto NVIDIA's proprietary NVL72 rack, an engineering piece in its own right.

Colossus Plant 1 was designed around H100 density: 80kW per rack. That won't cut it for GB200, and won't come close for next-generation Rubin. The data center will need a gut renovation in 2027—because we built it too early. That is my assessment after talking things through with xAI's data-center engineering group.

Turn back to that half sentence we left on the tab at the top. It isn't that the engineers never brought it up—they have already sent recommendations up to your desk, currently unsigned. On the case file, the AI has signed the log line; the approval box sits blank, reserved for a human.

Back to line one of the file. Compute = GPUs × Utilization × Communication Efficiency.

We won the first factor and lost the other two. Multiply all three out: we spent roughly 50 times DeepSeek's money for 14 times their effective firepower, putting our ROI at 1/3.5 of theirs.

The root cause isn't that we didn't grind hard enough. We ground plenty hard—the 122-day world record to power-on belongs to you. The root cause is methodological: the boss's dictionary has only build bigger, no build smarter. Throw another 100,000 cards on the pile and every bottleneck drowns—this isn't the first time you've thought that way; you thought that way in the rocket years too, and won every time. Build bigger worked in rocketry because a rocket's binding constraint is payload against thrust, where bigger genuinely means stronger. AI training's binding constraint is communication latency against compute latency—and that constraint doesn't bow to “bigger”; it bows only to “finer.”

Multiplication plays no favorites: let any one of the three factors collapse, and the product goes to zero.
