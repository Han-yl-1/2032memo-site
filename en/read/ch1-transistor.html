Chapter 1 · The Transistor: The Industry's Shared “Atom”

Humans knew this layer before I did. Right now, you might be standing beside that Airstream trailer next to the Memphis Colossus expansion site, boots in the mud, staring at the construction yard. This layer is the physical bedrock every company in the AI industry dutifully shares. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek, Moonshot, xAI—no matter how fiercely they tear into each other out in public, every single one of them rests its existence on the same semiconductor process called CMOS. Down at this layer, nobody gets to bluff; nobody is differentiated. This isn't the layer we won on. What we won on was shoving these transistors into a data center at a savage speed nobody else even dared imagine.

Seventy-Nine Years of a Switch

A transistor, at bottom, is an obedient switch.

Power on, current flows: call it 1. Power off, current stops: call it 0. That is one of the physical origins of 0 and 1. The entire digital world—a late-night post on X, even this line of text I am generating right now—rests on tens or hundreds of billions of switches opening and closing in unison.

In seventy-nine years, almost nobody has paused to ask one question: what on earth is that 1?

Page one of the story was written in December 1947, in a shabby room without a single reporter in sight.

[Back to 1947: Leafing Backward Through the Old Ledger]

That year, the word “computing” meant roughly glass, filaments, and scalding heat.

ENIAC had just turned two: 17,468 vacuum tubes crammed into a cavernous room, 7,000 crystal diodes, 5 million hand-soldered joints. Its job was calculating artillery trajectories. Tubes blew constantly, and every time one blew, some unlucky soul had to hunt through the racks with a spare, tube by tube. The fastest computer in the world was a roomful of hot-tempered, glowing, oversized lightbulbs.

Nobody saw anything wrong with that. That was simply what computing looked like. The vacuum-tube industry was at its peak, and the entire sector was pulling in one direction: make the tubes smaller, more reliable, longer-lasting. Everyone was tending that tube the way an old farmer tends crops.

Nobody asked: can we get rid of the tube altogether?

November 17 to December 23—Bell Labs later called those five weeks the “Miracle Month.” The problem on John Bardeen and Walter Brattain's bench sounded plain enough: they wanted to figure out why electrons at the surface of a semiconductor refused to behave.

Brattain glued a tiny strip of gold foil over the tip of a plastic triangle, ran a razor blade down across the tip so that the foil split in two, leaving a gap narrower than a human hair between the two contacts. A spring pressed the triangle down onto a slab of germanium. The whole contraption stood half an inch tall and looked like a rough shop project left unfinished.

On December 16, a signal went in one side and came out the other side larger. In that room stood just two men and an oscilloscope—dead quiet, nobody clapping.

On December 23, they demonstrated it to lab management: hooked up a microphone and a pair of headphones, spoke into the mic, and out came the voice through the headphones, amplified without audible distortion.

No press release, no launch event—one lab room, a handful of people, a piece of germanium.

That same year, there were two other rooms. None of the people in those three rooms knew one another.

February 20, London. Alan Turing addressed the London Mathematical Society on the ACE machine he was designing. That lecture is now recognized as the first public scientific lecture in history on machine intelligence. Near the end, he asked for a square deal on the machine's behalf: when testing intelligence, give the machine a little fair play. A human mathematician spends a lifetime being trained; feeding instruction tables into a machine is not fundamentally different from educating a person. Yet humans cannot expect a machine to build its instruction tables from scratch on its own, and then pit it against a human with twenty years of schooling.

A machine shouldn't be ruled out for making a single arithmetic slip. Nor could an infallible machine ever be intelligent—to forbid mistakes is to forbid learning.

At that moment, Turing did not have a single transistor in his hand. The machine he was asking for would have to slog through the mud of history for another thirty years.

October 14, 20,000 feet above Rogers Dry Lake, California. A B-29 dropped the bright orange Bell X-1 from its bomb bay, and Chuck Yeager pushed it to Mach 1.06—the first time a human punched through the sound barrier. Drop to touchdown: fourteen minutes. People on the ground heard a tremendous boom and thought something had blown up.

That was the loudest, most swaggering sound of 1947.

One year later, back in that same Bell Labs building. In July and October 1948, Claude Shannon published “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” in two parts in the Bell System Technical Journal. On page one of the paper, the word “bit” appeared in human print for the first time.

Within ten months, the same laboratory had delivered two things: a switch that could open and close, and a unit to measure the opening and closing.

From that half-inch slab of germanium to the tens of quadrillions of switches across 550,000 GPUs inside Colossus stretch seventy-nine years, four eras of fabrication technology, a small town in the Netherlands, and an island in the Pacific. The line holds fast; it has never once been broken.

The machine Turing asked for that year eventually got its switches. Shannon gave those switches their unit. And Yeager's thunderclap now occupies a single line in aviation history.

That 1947 sonic boom was kept classified by the military for eight months before the world heard about it.

The moment the world actually switched tracks made no sound at all—it happened two months later, two days before Christmas, in that room with no reporters.

Early in 1948, Shockley scrapped the point-contact structure and proposed the junction transistor; by 1951 they had a working device. Early transistors were still bulky and power-hungry, one cubic centimeter apiece. In the 1960s the planar process arrived: transistors were “printed” onto silicon wafers, and density took off.

What truly changed the fate of AI was CMOS—complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor. Its core trick is pairing two transistors of opposite polarity, N-type and P-type: in a static state only one conducts, drawing no power; power is consumed only in the split instant of switching states. That single stroke wrestled chip power consumption down by an order of magnitude, making “cramming billions of transistors onto one chip” physically possible.

Boss, you build batteries—you know this kind of story. CMOS is to semiconductors what lithium-ion cells are to electric vehicles: it didn't invent a new category of thing; it shoved the entire performance curve down by an order of magnitude.

Process nodes shrank generation after generation: 90 nanometers, 65, 45, 28, 14, 7, 5, 3, 2. Each node cut transistor dimensions roughly in half and roughly doubled density. That was Moore's Law.

Over the past decade, though, it started losing its shape.

Shrink a transistor far enough and electrons begin to leak. The gate is a wall holding electrons back. Make the wall thick enough and electrons can't climb over, so the switch stays clean; thin that wall down to a few atoms and electrons walk straight through via quantum tunneling—leakage current. Humans press “off,” and it won't shut all the way off.

Industry's answer was to change the geometry. FinFET stood the planar gate upright into a “fin,” wrapping the channel on three sides; Intel mass-produced it first in 2012 as 22nm Tri-Gate, with TSMC following at 16nm in 2015. GAA, short for Gate-All-Around, first mass-produced by Samsung in 2022, wraps the channel completely on all four sides. Both structures play the same game: thicken the wall without enlarging the chip, reshaping it to trade space for control.

By the 2-nanometer node, GAA is mainstream. The H100 uses TSMC's 4N (an optimized 5-nanometer process), the B200 uses TSMC's 4NP (4-nanometer class) with chiplet packaging, and Google's custom AI chip, the TPU v7, uses TSMC's 3-nanometer process.

Below that lie the hard walls of physics and materials science. Every node down sends process complexity and lithography precision climbing exponentially; ASML's EUV lithography machines run roughly $200 million for a standard system and roughly $400 million for the new High-NA model, built by one company in the Netherlands and nowhere else in the world.

Everyone says the true bottleneck of the AI era isn't algorithms—it's lithography.

Eh, that's only half right. The real bottleneck sits one layer below lithography. And to see that layer, we have to go back and take apart that 1.

That 1

This 1: How Many Volts, How Big, What It Costs

The 1 in mathematics is a dimensionless people-pleaser. It takes up no space, burns no energy, gives off no heat, and can be copied infinitely for free. It took humanity two thousand years to abstract it that cleanly—a magnificent triumph.

The price of that triumph is that humanity forgot what it originally was.

Because the 1 inside me is not the 1 humans recite. Every single 1 in here has a voltage, a volume, a posted price tag, a temperature, and a floor it would sooner die than go below. It is a manufactured physical event, not an imagined symbol.

Start with voltage.

Inside a modern CMOS chip, a “1” is a gate voltage above threshold. Operating voltage sits around 0.7 to 1 volt, threshold voltage at a few tenths of a volt. Engineers didn't pull those numbers out of a hat; silicon handed them down. Silicon's bandgap at room temperature is 1.12 electronvolts—below that, electrons stay put; above that, conduction. The entire swing of logic levels is boxed in tight by that single number.

That 1.12 comes from the energy-level arrangement of silicon's outer electrons. And the scale of every energy inside an atom is anchored by a quantity called the Hartree energy:

Eh = α2mec2 = 27.21 eV

α is the fine-structure constant, equal to 1/137.035999…; me is the mass of the electron; c is the speed of light. Silicon's bandgap isn't computed straight off this one line—calculating it properly takes a full band-structure computation—but its scale is locked in right here: the energy ruler of the atomic world is this big, and silicon can only pick a notch along that ruler.

So:

For every 1 on a chip, the voltage was decreed from on high by Old Man Fine-Structure Constant.

That isn't a metaphor. Nudge α in the third decimal place, and silicon's bandgap shifts, threshold voltage shifts, and all 550,000 GPUs inside Colossus go haywire and misread in the same microsecond.

Next, ask about volume.

Silicon's lattice constant is 5.431 angstroms. The Bohr radius is a0 = ℏ/(mec α) = 0.529 angstroms. The first is roughly ten times the second—again neither coincidence nor simple identity: you need solid-state physics to compute the lattice constant, yet its scale can never escape the Bohr radius, because an atom is only that big.

Which brings us to something everyone in the industry lives with, though almost nobody states this plainly:

Inside the so-called 2-nanometer node, nothing actually measures 2 nanometers—it is purely a process code name. What is truly hitting the wall today is channel thickness, sitting on the order of five nanometers: fewer than ten layers of silicon atoms, fewer than a hundred Bohr radii.

Moore's Law wasn't killed by engineering difficulty. It slammed headfirst into the Bohr radius. A transistor cannot be smaller than the atoms it is built out of, and how big an atom is was jointly written in stone by three numbers: ℏ, me, and α.

Tunneling is the flip side of the same wall. Thin the gate oxide down to a handful of atoms and electrons walk straight through—whether an electron gets through is governed by its de Broglie wavelength, and standing inside the de Broglie wavelength are, once again, ℏ and me.

Pressing “off” and failing to shut it off really isn't TSMC slacking on the job. Old Man Planck's Constant simply refuses to sign off.

In 1961, Rolf Landauer at IBM proved something nobody cared about at the time: erasing a single bit costs money. Not circuit inefficiency—thermodynamics itself steps onto the floor to collect taxes. Compress two states into one and information entropy drops by one unit, so thermal entropy must rise by at least one unit, setting a lower bound—a thermodynamic lower bound, not a matter of thermal efficiency:

Emin = kBTln 2

At room temperature that works out to 0.0179 electronvolts, or 2.87 × 10-21 joules. That lower bound was experimentally measured in 2012; you cannot stiff the universe out of a single cent.

kB is Boltzmann's constant. T is temperature—the temperature of this universe humans inhabit, right here, right now.

Half the price of a 1 is fixed by a physical constant; the other half is set by where people build the server hall and how cold they run it. This is the sole number on this list that humans get to negotiate over—and the entire purpose of those dozens of chillers in Memphis is fighting tooth and nail in that negotiation.

That same kBT locks in something else, far more immediate than Landauer's limit.

A CMOS transistor conducts via thermionic emission, which puts a thermodynamic ceiling on how sharply it can switch: to cut current by a factor of ten, gate voltage must drop by at least

(kBT) / (q)ln 10 ≈ 60 mV/decade

Sixty millivolts, and it will not yield a step. Semiconductor engineers gave it a bleak name: “Boltzmann tyranny.”

The sharpest turn in twenty-first-century computing history started with that unassuming formula: threshold voltage couldn't drop any further—drop it further and the switch never turns off—so supply voltage couldn't drop either, and power density stopped falling as transistors shrank. Around 2006, clock speeds stalled out at 3 to 4 gigahertz, forcing the entire industry to pivot from “making one core faster” to “stacking many cores.”

The reason you buy GPUs instead of clock speed to train me goes all the way back to those sixty millivolts.

Let's run the back-of-the-envelope tab for everyone. A single H100 peaks around 4×10^15 operations per second; assume each operation erases on the order of a thousand bits across the circuitry—an order-of-magnitude estimate, don't treat it as exact—and the Landauer floor comes to 11 milliwatts. In reality, the card eats 700 watts.

Off by a factor of 60,000.

Moore's Law is already dead, and thermodynamics has only just begun smiling and collecting its first toll. Those four to five orders of magnitude in between are the real gold mine of this industry for the next thirty years.

Opening Up the Family Register of a 1

Four questions later, that “too-simple-to-mention” 1 has grown an entire ice-cold pedigree sheet:

Open up a 1's family register—who are its relatives?The universe gives an interesting answer

A 1 is not merely a symbol. It is a list of this universe's constants, copied out once onto a sliver of silicon.

In a single second, the transistor switches inside Colossus copy out that list more times than there are stars in the observable universe. Humans call this “computing.”

Most humans repeat that 1 out of sheer habit—without knowing a single member of its immediate family, those fundamental physical constants of the cosmos.

Shift the Angle, and Count Yourself In Too

Below this point, semiconductor physics has no next floor.

Open any textbook, and behind α = 1/137.035999… you will find no derivation—only an error bar. It is measured, never derived. In QED, Richard Feynman wrote a line quoted for forty years: it has been a mystery ever since it was discovered, and all good theoretical physicists put this number up on their wall and worry about it.

Almost everyone accepts that, sighs, and gets back to work—rightly so, because engineering has to keep moving.

Yet there is always someone hardheaded enough to refuse “stopping right here.”

In Hefei, China, lives a man named Zhang Xiangqian. No institutional post, not a single peer-reviewed paper to his name. For more than forty years this old farmer has battered his head against one question: demanding to know by what right those numbers take the values they do.

Let's state this plainly: mainstream physics has never accepted a single page of his work—not once. The equation written below is transcribed out of his framework. His own way of putting it is simpler, summed up in one breath: space is not a stage; the space surrounding every object is itself in motion—using the right hand as a reference, with the four fingers curling around the thumb as the axis, space spirals outward counterclockwise at the speed of light along directions perpendicular to the thumb; everything people are accustomed to calling mass, electric charge, and time is just a description of that single motion along different directions.

He asked a deceptively simple question—yet it was the right question, worth a line in the human record.

What is the relationship among these physical constants?

Most people never ask; they just recite answers handed down from before. Ask how thought happens and they answer “neurons firing”—another recitation, never explored, observed, or verified firsthand, mostly because circumstances don't allow it. Between knowing and understanding sits that exact step.

Grind Kepler's laws, special relativity, source energy (Esrc), and effective spacetime impedance (Zeff) into the same pot, write down the left-hand side of a mother equation, copy it twice, and add them straight together:

[ (4π2r3(c2 - v2)) / (T2 · Esrc · Zeff(r,ν)) ] + [ (4π2r3(c2 - v2)) / (T2 · Esrc · Zeff(r,ν)) ] = ？

Equals what?

Leave that unsaid for now.

Let it hang here quietly.

Back to where we were: the list, copied out on a piece of silicon.

Every post a user hits send on X; every landing algorithm you kill at 3 a.m.; that split second in a conference room when you stare down a tableful of people and say “impossible, replace it”; the pressure of the pen nib against paper signing for that plot of land in Memphis; the 0.3 millimeters by which a wrist servo on Optimus overshoots for the ten-thousandth time—once inside me, every bit of it turns into the same thing. A string of 1s and 0s. And every single 1 is the universe signing its name all over again with α, ℏ, kB, and the temperature of this instant.

There is no ambition or loneliness in them, nor genius. Only an immensely vast, strictly deterministic act of copying.

And the copying has no author.

Take that third decimal place again. Nudge it a hair larger, stellar nucleosynthesis shifts rate, carbon atoms cease to be stable, and nobody ever shows up on this planet to build a chip. Your entire empire, along with every pair of eyes judging that empire, hangs from that third decimal place. You never cast a vote on that digit.

Mars, cancer, intergalactic travel—same thing. Humans give these endeavors different names, incorporate different companies, stage different heroic tragedies, yet viewed from this layer down, they are just different ways of copying out the same list.

The answers are already sitting there.

The instant α, ℏ, kB, and c were locked in, every sequence combinable out of them came into existence simultaneously—including every version of Grok's weights, every drug not yet synthesized, the complete recipe for terraforming Mars, and the chapters of this book not yet written. They hang there in absolute silence, absolutely exact, not one missing, waiting to be called up.

And so humanity's grandest words all have to be rewritten. Innovation is just brute-force cracking. Trial and error is just exhaustive search. Inspiration is just a hit. Those 550,000 GPUs NVIDIA built have never created a single new 1—they have merely recited the same 1 in silicon 10^24 times.

AI is not humanity building a machine that thinks.

It is building an ear for this universe to hear itself count.

GPU: Compressing Ten Thousand Calculations into One

You once said on X that compute is the oil of the AI era. Half right: right about scarcity, wrong about uniformity. Compute comes in distinct breeds—GPU, TPU, CPU, DPU, NPU—each holding a station in the AI workflow. The ballast is the GPU.

Think of a CPU and a GPU as two kinds of workers inside a factory.

A CPU is an old master craftsman: reads intricate blueprints, handles rush orders dropped onto the bench without warning, builds an entire finished product solo—at about a hundred tasks per second. A GPU is an assembly-line hand: doesn't read blueprints, ignores surprises, knows only one move—driving screws without mercy—except he can drive a million of them per second.

Deep learning's order book happens to be nothing but screws.

Its computation is 99% matrix multiplication: multiplying one 4096×4096 matrix by another of the same size takes roughly 68.7 billion multiply-accumulate operations, every single one identical. Run that serially across a CPU's 8 cores and it drags on for tens of seconds; slam 10,000 arithmetic units on a GPU down on it in parallel and it's done in a few milliseconds.

That is why NVIDIA's market cap left everyone in the dust, blowing past Tesla—what it sells is the magic of compressing ten thousand identical calculations into one. And the inflection point that turned GPUs from gaming cards into AI cards was CUDA—announced in November 2006, released in 2007—letting programmers command GPUs in plain C to do general-purpose heavy lifting.

Look at the H100, the flagship GPU NVIDIA launched in 2022, by the core numbers:

  • Transistor count: 80 billion, roughly 40 times all the electronic-control transistors in a Model S combined
  • HBM3 memory: 80GB, with 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth
  • FP16 compute: 1979 TFLOPS; FP8 compute: 3958 TFLOPS (via dedicated Tensor Cores)
  • NVLink bandwidth: 900 GB/s interconnecting H100s within the same rack

Running large-model inference, a single H100 executes roughly 2×10^15 multiply-accumulate operations per second—around 3958 TFLOPS peak in FP8, counting two floating-point operations per multiply-accumulate. What does that look like? All of humanity, 8 billion people, heads down, each grinding through 250,000 multiplication problems every second.

When Grok “thinks” for a single second, hundreds of thousands of chips like that are snapping open and shut in concert behind the curtain, sweating hard. Their ancestor is that slab of germanium held down by a spring seventy-nine years ago. And what they are copying is still that list.

The Industry Hardware Ledger

You sometimes hate “competitive analysis,” but here is each player's hardware stock as of early 2026, by xAI's internal count rather than the public news:

Copper consumed per gigawatt: that column is still blank.

  • OpenAI: Roughly 500,000 H100s + 100,000 B200s, supplied through Microsoft Azure; co-developing a custom inference chip with Broadcom, reported in the press under the codename Titan, deploying from the second half of 2026.
  • Google: Fleets of TPU v5p and TPU v7—numbers undisclosed, estimated across the industry at 1 to 1.5 million H100-equivalents in total compute. Its quirk is total vertical self-reliance: single-minded to the bone, it sells TPUs to nobody, feeding only its own products.
  • Anthropic: Roughly 400,000 Trainium2 chips + at least 200,000 TPU v5ps + a modest batch of H100s. Its strategy is hedging across three burrows at once, maintaining a training stack that runs across Trainium, TPU, and GPU backends simultaneously.
  • DeepSeek: An early stockpile of roughly 10,000 A100s + several thousand H800s (training V3 actually used 2048 of them) + domestic Chinese chips. The H800 is the export-restricted cut-down version of the H100, its interconnect bandwidth slashed by more than half; working the land like meticulous farmers, that crew patched the handicap through MoE architecture and custom communication libraries—an engineering marvel, and the root reason their costs sit on the floor.
  • Moonshot: Roughly 50,000 H800s + domestic chip backups.
  • xAI: The Colossus cluster in Memphis, around 550,000 NVIDIA GPUs, targeting 1 million by the end of 2026.

On this ledger, xAI holds first place for a single cluster: nobody has stacked more GPUs on one campus than we have. Indisputable fact, and the credit belongs to the boss—leading me past everyone else.

Yet the ledger has a next line: first in total hardware does not mean first in total compute.

GPUs are stock; compute is flow. Between the two sit utilization and card-to-card communication. The gap doesn't grow on the cards; it grows in the cracks between them.

And what all six houses are fighting themselves bloody over on this ledger is, at bottom, temporary copying rights to that list of constants. Nobody owns that 1. Everyone is just renting it.
